\begin{abstract}
First-generation AI weather models are often reported to underperform at
extremes, mostly in reanalysis-based evaluations of deterministic regression
systems. We verify twelve physical and AI forecast systems against European
synoptic, solar and rain-gauge stations over ten months for 10\,m wind,
2\,m temperature, hourly shortwave accumulation and hourly precipitation,
scoring MAE against ECMWF IFS in ERA5 1991--2020 climatological regimes.
Among these systems, AI models do not show a uniform relative-skill deficit
in the tails. Jua EPT-2.1 Europa leads all-conditions wind ($+8.4$\%), while
Jua EPT-2 HRRR leads temperature overall ($+12.1$\%) and in the heat regime
($+19.6\pm2.2$\%). EPT-2.1 Europa and DWD ICON Global lead at gale-force
wind. Jua EPT-2.1 Helios leads solar overall ($+10.2\pm1.7$\%), in overcast
conditions ($+16.4\pm3.4$\%), and in the clear-sky tail
($+24.8\pm5.4$\%). For precipitation, three Jua models gain 14--15\% at
moderate intensity and 9--11\% at P75--P95; EPT-2 Reasoning remains ahead
at $>$P95 ($+1.7\pm0.5$\%). Failures are model-specific: ECMWF AIFS loses
$4.9\pm2.0$\% in the heat tail and its CRPS-trained ensemble AIFS ENS,
scored on the ensemble mean, loses $5.1\pm1.7$\% there, while NOAA GFS
loses $22.8\pm2.0$\%.
Every model, NWPs included, shows a shared conditional bias toward the centre
of the observed distribution, with an inter-model spread several times
smaller than the shared signal. Missing relative skill at extremes is
therefore not a property of AI weather models as a class, but of particular
AI and physical models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Data-driven weather models now outperform operational physical models on
many standard forecast metrics
\citep{bi2023pangu,lam2023graphcast,bouallegue2024rise}. Their performance
at extremes, however, remains an open concern. Models trained with
mean-squared error tend towards the conditional mean, which can smooth
sharp gradients and attenuate rare events
\citep{olivetti2024,bonavita2024limitations}. Existing evaluations support
this concern, reporting underestimated peak winds, hot and windy tails,
and record-breaking events
\citep{charltonperez2024,olivetti2024extremes,zhang2026records}.

The available evidence has two important limitations. First, it is
dominated by first-generation deterministic regression models. Generative
ensembles, probabilistically trained models, and high-resolution regional
AI models have received much less attention. Second, most studies verify
forecasts against reanalysis rather than direct station observations.
Whether their conclusions extend to newer model classes and independent
in situ measurements is therefore unclear.

Two questions are often conflated in this discussion: whether forecasts
systematically attenuate extreme observations, and whether AI models are
less accurate than physical models under the same conditions. The first
concerns absolute forecast error; the second concerns relative skill. A
model may underestimate an extreme event while still producing a smaller
error than its physical counterpart.

We examine these questions using thirteen forecast models, including ECMWF
IFS as the reference. Forecasts are verified over ten months against
European synoptic, solar-radiation, and rain-gauge observations. The
evaluation covers 10\,m wind, 2\,m temperature, hourly solar radiation,
and hourly precipitation at lead times up to 48\,h. Models are compared
both overall and within regimes defined by the observed value relative to
a fixed 1991--2020 climatology.

Throughout the paper, ``extreme'' refers to the tails of this local
climatological distribution, principally observations below the 5th or
above the 95th quantile. These regimes identify conditions that are
unusual for a given location, calendar day, and UTC hour; they are not
necessarily record-breaking or impact-defined events. Precipitation is
treated separately within the same framework by distinguishing dry hours
and quantiles of the wet-hour distribution
\citep{zhang2011indices,perkins2013heatwaves}.

The analysis reveals a common tendency among AI and physical models to
overpredict low observations and underpredict high observations. Relative
skill within those regimes, however, varies substantially by model and
variable. This distinction is central: attenuation of extreme values does
not imply a uniform AI-specific deficit relative to physical forecasting.

The study makes three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item It extends station-based evaluation of weather extremes to
deterministic regression models, a CRPS-trained global ensemble, and
high-resolution regional generative models.

\item It separates relative MAE within climatological regimes from the
conditional forecast errors observed in those regimes.

\item It tests whether these patterns persist across four surface
variables, forecast lead times, countries, and model classes, with
additional categorical evaluation of heavy precipitation.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the verification protocol.


\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{AI weather models at extremes.}
Evidence of attenuated extremes has been reported in both case studies and
systematic evaluations. For storm Ciar\'an,
\citet{charltonperez2024} found that FourCastNet, FourCastNet-v2,
Pangu-Weather, and GraphCast reproduced the track and central pressure but
underestimated peak winds by approximately 8\,m\,s\(^{-1}\). The same
bias was not present when the models were initialised from ERA5, suggesting
that smoothing in the training data was not its sole cause; the role of
the verification target remained unresolved.

Across a broader sample, \citet{olivetti2024extremes} found Pangu-Weather
and GraphCast competitive with IFS HRES for many regional extremes but
weaker for hot and windy tails at longer leads.
\citet{zhang2026records} similarly reported HRES ahead of GraphCast,
Pangu-Weather, and FuXi for record-breaking events defined from ERA5.
\citet{bonavita2024limitations} associated this behaviour with an inability
to reproduce sub-synoptic variability, arguing that deterministic AI
forecasts can resemble statistical post-processing more closely than
free-running simulation. A station-based evaluation by
\citet{bouallegue2024rise} reached a more qualified conclusion:
Pangu-Weather remained accurate for moderate extremes but smoothed the
largest events. Collectively, this evidence is drawn mainly from
deterministic regression models introduced between 2022 and 2024 and
verified primarily against reanalysis.

\paragraph{Training objectives and generative models.}
Several studies connect attenuation to the training objective rather than
to data-driven forecasting itself. \citet{subich2025doublepenalty}
attributed GraphCast's loss of effective resolution to the double penalty
induced by grid-point mean-squared error. Replacing this objective with an
amplitude-preserving loss reduced the estimated effective scale from
approximately 1{,}250 to 160\,km and improved surface-wind extremes.
In turbulent-flow experiments, \citet{molinaro2024gencfd} found that
ensembles of mean-squared-error models converged towards the mean flow,
whereas score-based diffusion preserved the relevant statistics and
spectra.

Generative weather models provide further evidence that tail deficits are
not inevitable. GenCast outperformed the physical ECMWF ensemble on
extremes verified against ERA5 \citep{price2024gencast}, while StormCast
demonstrated kilometre-scale diffusion emulation of a regional
convection-allowing model \citep{pathak2026stormcast}. Direct
station-based comparisons between these models, deterministic AI
forecasts, and physical models nevertheless remain limited.

\paragraph{Station-based verification.}
WeatherReal \citep{jin2024weatherreal} and MAUSAM
\citep{gupta2025mausam} provide in situ benchmarks for data-driven weather
forecasting. MAUSAM reports errors against observations that are
15--45\% larger than those measured against reanalysis, indicating that
reanalysis-based evaluation can overstate forecast skill. We build on
this line of work by evaluating climatological tail regimes directly
against station observations using the StationBench verification
framework \citep{wagner2025stationbench}.


\section{Methods}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Models}

We evaluate twelve forecast models relative to the ECMWF IFS
high-resolution deterministic forecast (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The model
set spans physical and data-driven forecasting, global and regional
domains, and deterministic and ensemble prediction.

The physical models comprise ECMWF ENS, NOAA GFS, DWD ICON Global, and
DWD ICON-EU. The AI models include global regression models (ECMWF AIFS,
Microsoft Aurora, and Jua EPT-2 Reasoning), global ensembles (ECMWF AIFS
ENS and Jua EPT-2e), regional generative ensembles (Jua EPT-2 HRRR and
EPT-2.1 Europa), and the solar-specialised regional model EPT-2.1 Helios
\citep{lang2024aifs,bodnar2025aurora,molinaro2025ept2,
lang2024aifscrps,jua2025hrrr,jua2026europadocs,jua2026helios}.
Unlike deterministic AIFS, AIFS ENS is trained with a continuous ranked
probability score objective and learned noise injection and is evaluated
as a 51-member ensemble. The methodology underlying the regional
generative models is described by
\citet{molinaro2026downscaling,molinaro2024gencfd}.

Models are evaluated only for variables with compatible outputs.
Precipitation is available from the physical models and from EPT-2
Reasoning, EPT-2e, EPT-2 HRRR, and EPT-2.1 Europa. AIFS ENS is restricted
to 10\,m wind and 2\,m temperature because its radiation and precipitation
outputs cannot be matched to the hourly accumulations used for
verification.

\subsection{Station observations and evaluation}
\label{sec:stations}

\paragraph{Station data.}
Forecasts are verified against three complementary in situ networks over
Europe:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Synoptic network:} quality-controlled observations of
10\,m wind and 2\,m temperature.

\item \textbf{Solar-radiation network:} hourly global horizontal irradiance
(GHI) from DWD, M\'et\'eo-France, MeteoSwiss, KNMI, RMI, DMI, MET Norway
(Frost), GeoSphere Austria, SMHI, FMI, BSRN, and ARPA Lombardia.

\item \textbf{Rain-gauge network:} hourly precipitation from 4{,}005 gauges
operated by DWD, M\'et\'eo-France, MET Norway, AEMET, GeoSphere Austria,
the UK Met Office, KNMI, RMI, CHMI, DMI, and MeteoSwiss. A separate
rain-gauge network is required because the synoptic feed does not provide
usable hourly precipitation accumulations.
\end{itemize}

The evaluation domain comprises thirteen countries: DE, FR, GB, ES, IT,
PL, NL, BE, CH, NO, AT, CZ, and DK. Precipitation coverage excludes Italy
and Poland. Gridded forecasts are bilinearly interpolated in latitude and
longitude to the observation locations
\citep{molinaro2026downscaling}. Precipitation is scored only where gauges
fall within a model's forecast domain; regional models are compared using
a common gauge sample within each evaluation cell.

None of the Jua models was trained or fine-tuned on these observations.
Historical station errors are used only for the causal bias correction
described in Section~\ref{sec:debias}.

\paragraph{Evaluation tracks.}
Differences in model availability and output cadence motivate two
complementary evaluation tracks:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Track A} covers 1 September 2025 to 30 June 2026. Wind and
temperature are evaluated for IFS and the ten comparator models available
throughout this period, with ICON-EU reserved for Track~B. The common lead
grid runs from 6 to 48\,h at 6-hourly intervals, matching the output
cadence of AIFS, AIFS ENS, and Aurora.

Solar radiation is evaluated over the same period from 1 to 48\,h at
hourly intervals. EPT-2.1 Helios is included, whereas AIFS, AIFS ENS,
Aurora, and ECMWF ENS are omitted because they lack compatible hourly
solar output. The solar network has no stations in GB, ES, or PL. Italian
coverage, provided mainly by ARPA Lombardia, ends in February 2026, so
solar results from March onwards exclude Italy.

Precipitation is evaluated over the same 1 to 48\,h range for models with
compatible output. Models with hourly forecasts contribute to the complete
lead grid. ECMWF ENS contributes only at its native 6-hourly leads and
should therefore be interpreted as a lower-cadence comparator in aggregate
results.

\item \textbf{Track B} covers 1 March to 30 June 2026 and compares
EPT-2.1 Europa, EPT-2 HRRR, and ICON-EU over a common period and hourly
lead grid. Wind, temperature, solar radiation, and precipitation are
evaluated from 1 to 48\,h. EPT-2.1 Helios is additionally included in the
solar comparison.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Observed-value regimes}
\label{sec:thresholds}

Regimes are defined relative to the fixed 1991--2020 ERA5 climatology
\citep{hersbach2020era5}, rather than to the evaluation period. ERA5
fields are bilinearly interpolated from the \(0.25^\circ\) grid to each
station. For every station, calendar day, and UTC hour, values are drawn
from a cyclic \(\pm15\)-day window over the 30-year reference period. The
window contains 930 climatological values before variable-specific
conditioning and preserves both seasonal and diurnal variability.

The reference distribution depends on the support of the variable. Wind
and temperature use all climatological values. Solar radiation and
precipitation both contain a structural mass at zero and require
conditioning. Solar thresholds are estimated from positive daytime GHI.
For precipitation, observations below \(0.1\)~mm form a separate dry
regime, while the remaining thresholds are estimated from wet hours only.

We write \(q_\alpha(s,d,h)\) for the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the relevant
reference distribution at station \(s\), calendar day \(d\), and UTC hour
\(h\). Wind, temperature, and solar radiation use
\(q_{0.05}\), \(q_{0.25}\), \(q_{0.75}\), and \(q_{0.95}\).
Precipitation uses the wet-hour quantiles
\(q_{0.50}^{\mathrm{wet}}\), \(q_{0.75}^{\mathrm{wet}}\), and
\(q_{0.95}^{\mathrm{wet}}\). The dependence on station, day, and hour is
omitted below for readability.

Each forecast--observation pair is assigned to a regime according to its
observed value \(y\). Regime membership is therefore identical across
models and lead times. The meteorological labels in
Tables~\ref{tab:regimes} and \ref{tab:precipregimes} are shorthand for
the corresponding climatological intervals.

Defining extremes through exceedances of a fixed local climatology follows
established practice in the climate-extremes literature
\citep{zhang2011indices,kleintank2009guidelines,
perkins2013heatwaves,perkins2015review}. The 1991--2020 reference period
follows the WMO climate-normal convention. Conditioning precipitation
quantiles on wet hours is consistent with ETCCDI wet-day indices such as
R95p. We extend the same climatological framework to wind and solar
radiation.

Because the thresholds are estimated independently of the evaluation
period, regime frequencies need not match their nominal quantile
probabilities. Moreover, the lower spatial variance of gridded ERA5
relative to point observations makes the wind and solar tails broader than
those obtained from a station-based climatology.

For wind and temperature, the upper tail is the primary regime of
interest. For solar radiation, the lower and upper tails correspond to
overcast and near-clear-sky conditions. Lead-time figures focus on the
overcast \(q_{0.05}\)--\(q_{0.25}\) regime, whereas regime-level results
cover the full distribution.

\subsection{Bias correction}
\label{sec:debias}

Station-based verification can be affected by systematic offsets that are
routinely removed in operational post-processing. We therefore apply a
causal rolling correction to each model before computing the reported
scores.

For wind, temperature, and daytime solar radiation, the correction is
additive. Let \(w\) denote a valid week, \(h\) the UTC hour of the valid
time, and \(\mathcal{D}_{w,h}\) the matched forecast--observation pairs at
hour \(h\) during the preceding four weeks. For model \(m\), the estimated
bias is
\begin{equation}
\bar b_{m,w,h}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{w,h}|}
\sum_{(f_m,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{w,h}}
(f_m-y),
\end{equation}
and forecasts valid during week \(w\) are corrected according to
\begin{equation}
\widetilde f_m(t)
=
f_m(t)-\bar b_{m,w,h(t)}.
\end{equation}
The estimate is pooled across European stations and does not depend on
initialisation or lead time. The procedure is identical for every model,
although the estimated bias is model-specific. For ensemble models, the
same shift is applied to every member, preserving the ensemble spread.

Precipitation retains a substantial probability mass at zero and has a
strongly right-skewed distribution, making an additive correction
unsuitable. Let \(\mathcal{P}_w\) contain precipitation forecasts and
observations from the preceding four weeks, pooled across initialisation
hours and lead times. Forecasts valid during week \(w\) are multiplied by
\begin{equation}
a_{m,w}
=
\operatorname{clip}_{[0.33,\,3]}
\left(
\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}y}
     {\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}f_m}
\right),
\qquad
\widetilde f_m(t)=a_{m,w}f_m(t).
\end{equation}
This correction adjusts systematic wet or dry amount biases while
preserving zero forecasts. All precipitation results use the
multiplicatively corrected forecasts.

Both corrections use only information available before the valid week.
Because regimes are assigned from observations, bias correction does not
alter regime membership. The correction removes the recent mean bias but
does not force the conditional bias within each regime to vanish.

\subsection{Skill, lead times, and aggregation}
\label{sec:aggregation}

Let \(m\) denote a forecast model and \(c\) an evaluation cell, specified
by the track, country, variable, lead time, and regime. We define the MAE
skill of model \(m\) relative to ECMWF IFS as
\begin{equation}
S_{m,c}
=
1-\frac{\operatorname{MAE}_{m,c}}
        {\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{IFS},c}}.
\end{equation}
Positive values indicate lower MAE than IFS. Within each evaluation cell,
the numerator and denominator are computed from the same
forecast--observation pairs, using the common intersection of available
initialisations across the models being compared and the four daily cycles
at 00, 06, 12, and 18 UTC.

For ECMWF ENS, ECMWF AIFS ENS, Jua EPT-2e, EPT-2 HRRR, and EPT-2.1
Europa, the ensemble mean is used as the point forecast. The analysis
therefore assesses point-forecast accuracy rather than ensemble spread or
probabilistic calibration. Mean forecast-minus-observation error is also
reported within each regime.

Results are reported at 1, 6, 12, 24, and 48\,h, where available, and
aggregated over the full lead-time grid of each comparison. Track~A wind
and temperature use 6-hourly leads from 6 to 48\,h. Track~A solar and
Track~B use hourly leads from 1 to 48\,h, as do precipitation models with
hourly output. Lower-cadence models contribute only at their native leads;
no temporal interpolation is applied.

Uncertainty is estimated using a leave-one-month-out jackknife and reported
as one standard error around each point estimate. For precipitation, we
also evaluate exceedance of the local \(q_{0.95}^{\mathrm{wet}}\)
threshold. Forecast and observed exceedances are pooled across gauges and
lead times up to 48\,h to compute the probability of detection (POD),
false-alarm ratio (FAR), critical success index (CSI), and frequency bias,
defined as the ratio of forecast to observed event counts.

In lead-time figures, colour identifies the model. Solid lines denote the
generative Jua models and the CRPS-trained AIFS ENS, dash--dot lines denote
regression AI models, and dashed lines denote physical models. ECMWF IFS
is shown as a solid black reference.

\label{sec:leads}

Figure~\ref{fig:preciplead} report MAE skill relative to ECMWF IFS by lead

figure shows both all-conditions performance and the corresponding tail

The leading wind and temperature models remain stable across the forecast
horizon. EPT-2.1 Europa retains a consistent advantage for wind, while
EPT-2 HRRR leads for temperature without a clear deterioration towards
48\,h.

AIFS ENS improves substantially over deterministic AIFS for wind under all
conditions, approaching IFS at longer leads. This improvement does not
extend to gale-force winds, where both variants remain clearly below the
reference. A similar distinction appears for temperature: their overall

largest advantage in the short range. Precipitation shows greater
lead-time dependence: positive skill among the Jua models generally

\subsection{Skill across the observed distribution}
\label{sec:regimes}

Figures~\ref{fig:bars} and \ref{fig:precipbars} report Track~A MAE skill
across the observed distribution. Figure~\ref{fig:bars} covers wind,
temperature, and solar radiation, with values given in
Tables~\ref{tab:skillwind}--\ref{tab:skillsolar}.
Figure~\ref{fig:precipbars} and Table~\ref{tab:precipskill} provide the
corresponding precipitation results. Short-range results over 1--12\,h
are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:bars12} and \ref{fig:precipbars12} and
Tables~\ref{tab:skillshort}--\ref{tab:skillshortsolar} and
\ref{tab:precipskillshort}. Together, these comparisons cover the full
observed distribution, including very-low and dry conditions.

For wind, EPT-2.1 Europa performs most strongly in the centre of the
distribution and retains positive skill at gale force. In the upper tail,
its point estimate is close to that of ICON Global, showing that the
leading AI and physical models are comparably accurate in this regime.
For temperature, EPT-2 HRRR has the highest overall and heat-regime
estimates, followed closely by EPT-2.1 Europa; the difference between them
is small relative to the jackknife uncertainty.

similar deficits in calm, gale-force, severe-cold, and heat conditions.
The CRPS objective and ensemble averaging therefore improve the bulk of
the AIFS distribution without eliminating its tail errors. NOAA GFS has
the largest temperature deficit, with negative heat skill in every country

\subsection{Shared conditional-error pattern}
\label{sec:fingerprint}

Figure~\ref{fig:fingerprint} reports mean forecast-minus-observation error
at 48\,h, conditioned on the observed-value regime. The grey envelope
shows the range across all models; selected models are highlighted to make
their differences visible.

Despite their different architectures and relative skill, all models show
the same centre-seeking pattern: they overpredict low observations and
underpredict high observations. The variation across regimes is much
larger than the spread between models. The effect is strongest for wind
and precipitation, with gale-force winds underpredicted by more than
2\,m\,s\(^{-1}\) and the heaviest precipitation by approximately
2\,mm. Temperature and solar radiation show the same change in error sign,
although with smaller absolute differences.

Because the regimes are defined by the verifying observation, this pattern
is not direct evidence of smoothing. It can also arise from limited
predictability, unresolved local variability, observation and
representativeness errors, or regression towards the conditional mean.
The relevant finding is that this common conditional structure coexists
with model-specific differences in relative MAE.

\section{Discussion}

The results separate two distinct meanings of ``missing extremes.'' First,
a forecast may underestimate the magnitude of an extreme observation.
Second, it may be less accurate than a competing model under the same
conditions. The first behaviour is common across the evaluated models:
low observations tend to be overpredicted and high observations
underpredicted. The second is not. Several AI models achieve lower MAE
than IFS within the heat, gale, solar, and precipitation regimes.
Attenuation of extreme values therefore does not imply a general
AI-specific loss of relative skill.

This distinction helps place the results in the context of earlier work.
Previous studies largely considered first-generation regression models
verified against reanalysis and found competitive average performance
alongside underestimation of event magnitude
\citep{olivetti2024extremes}. The present station-based evaluation includes
a broader range of models and reveals substantial variation across both
models and variables. EPT-2.1 Helios performs strongly in the two solar
tails but poorly in the typical irradiance regime. The Jua precipitation
models improve most clearly through the middle of the wet distribution,
while their advantage narrows at the highest intensities. Relative skill
at extremes is therefore not determined by overall skill alone.

The differences are also poorly described by a simple distinction between
AI and physical models. AIFS exhibits deficits in the wind and temperature
tails, and these persist for AIFS ENS despite its improved performance in
the centre of the distribution. By contrast, the ensemble means of
EPT-2 HRRR and EPT-2.1 Europa remain competitive at gale force and in
heat. This cannot be explained by ensemble-mean evaluation alone: the
ECMWF ENS mean also loses skill in the heat regime. The largest heat
deficit belongs to the physical GFS model, while the physical ICON-EU is
comparable with the leading regional AI models in the wind and temperature
tails. For precipitation, EPT-2 HRRR is the only regional model with
positive skill from light through high rain.

These comparisons do not identify the cause of the observed differences.
The models vary simultaneously in architecture, training objective,
resolution, training data, initialisation, forecast cadence, and spatial
domain \citep{bonavita2024limitations,subich2025doublepenalty}. Performance
cannot therefore be attributed to a single distinction such as AI versus
physics, regression versus generative training, or global versus regional
resolution. Moreover, evaluating ensembles through their means measures
point-forecast accuracy but provides no evidence about calibration,
spread, or extreme-event probabilities.

The shared conditional-error pattern requires similar caution. Because
regimes are defined from the verifying observation, overprediction at low
values and underprediction at high values can arise from limited
predictability, unresolved local variability, representativeness and
observation errors, or regression towards the conditional mean. It should
not be interpreted as direct evidence of model smoothing. Nevertheless,
the pattern is relevant because it coexists with clear differences in
relative MAE. At the heaviest precipitation intensities, for example,
EPT-2 Reasoning remains slightly ahead of IFS, EPT-2.1 Europa is close to
it, and EPT-2 HRRR, EPT-2e, and GFS fall below it.

Absolute attenuation and relative skill are therefore complementary
properties. The evaluated AI models can underestimate extreme observations
without being uniformly less accurate than physical models, while strong
average performance does not guarantee an advantage in every tail.
Within this evaluation, performance at extremes is best understood as a
property of individual models and variables rather than of AI weather
forecasting as a single class.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The regime thresholds are estimated from ERA5 rather than from station
records. An ``extreme'' therefore denotes a value that is unusual relative
to the local ERA5 1991--2020 climatology, conditioned on calendar day and
UTC hour; it does not necessarily represent an impact-defined heatwave,
gale, or record event. Because ERA5 represents grid-cell averages, it has
lower variance than point observations. The wind and solar tails
consequently contain approximately 10\% of observations each, making them
less selective than the corresponding temperature tails.

Precipitation is evaluated against a separate rain-gauge panel covering
eleven countries, with Italy and Poland absent. Its thresholds are derived
from the ERA5 wet-hour distribution and therefore retain the same mismatch
between gridded climatology and point observations. Hourly gauge
accumulations are also noisier than the synoptic wind and temperature
measurements. The heavy-precipitation results should consequently be
interpreted as a relative comparison with IFS on this station panel, not
as an absolute assessment of convective-scale forecast skill.

Although the ten-month evaluation contains many forecast--observation
pairs, it does not span a complete annual cycle and samples a limited set
of meteorological events. It supports the climatological regime
comparisons reported here but cannot characterise every form of rare,
persistent, compound, or record-breaking weather.

Ensemble models are evaluated through their means. The study therefore
measures point-forecast accuracy and does not assess calibration, spread,
event probabilities, or other aspects of probabilistic forecast quality.
Nor can differences between regression, generative, and physical models be
attributed to their training objectives: the models also differ in
resolution, inputs, architecture, initialisation, domain, and forecast
cadence.

The reported results use a common causal bias-correction procedure
estimated from the preceding four weeks of station errors. They compare
models after operational post-processing rather than entirely raw
forecasts. In addition, ECMWF ENS contributes to the precipitation
comparison only at its native 6-hourly leads, whereas the other
precipitation models are evaluated hourly.

\section{Conclusion}

AI weather models do not exhibit a uniform MAE deficit relative to ECMWF
IFS in the observed-value tails considered here. In Track~A, EPT-2.1
Europa leads for wind, EPT-2 HRRR for temperature, and EPT-2.1 Helios for
solar radiation. The largest precipitation gains occur at moderate
intensities and diminish towards the heavy tail.

The counterexamples are equally important. AIFS and AIFS ENS remain below
IFS in the wind and heat tails, GFS has the largest heat deficit, and
EPT-2.1 Helios loses skill in the centre of the solar distribution.
Track~B yields the same model-dependent picture: Europa and ICON-EU are
comparable at gale force and in heat, while EPT-2 HRRR performs strongly
across light to high rain but not at the heaviest intensities.

Every evaluated model nevertheless overpredicts low observations and
underpredicts high observations after bias correction. Models can
therefore attenuate extreme values without being uniformly less accurate
than IFS within those regimes. In this evaluation, performance at extremes
is a property of individual models and variables, not a general
distinction between AI and physical weather forecasting.
