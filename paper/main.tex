\title{Do AI weather models miss extremes?\\
Evidence from ten months of verification against European
weather stations}

\begin{abstract}
  AI weather models are often reported to underestimate extremes, but most
  evidence concerns deterministic regression models verified against
  reanalysis. We evaluate twelve physical and AI forecast models against
  ECMWF IFS using ten months of European station observations. The
  evaluation covers 10\,m wind, 2\,m temperature, solar radiation, and
  precipitation within regimes defined from a fixed ERA5 1991--2020
  climatology. We find no uniform AI-specific deficit in the tails. Several
  AI models remain more accurate than IFS under extreme conditions, while
  others deteriorate markedly; comparable variation occurs among physical
  models. Every model nevertheless exhibits a common conditional-error
  pattern, overpredicting low observations and underpredicting high
  observations. Attenuation of extreme values therefore does not imply a
  uniform loss of relative skill: tail performance depends on the model,
  variable, and evaluation setting rather than on whether the forecast is
  produced by AI or physical numerical modelling.
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Data-driven weather models have achieved strong performance on standard
forecast metrics, often matching or surpassing operational numerical
weather prediction (NWP) models
\citep{bi2023pangu,lam2023graphcast,bouallegue2024rise}. However, many of
these models produce deterministic forecasts and are trained using
mean-squared error. These models tend to regress towards the conditional
mean, smoothing small-scale variability and underestimating extremes, as
reported for peak winds and record-breaking events
\citep{olivetti2024,bonavita2024limitations,charltonperez2024,
olivetti2024extremes,zhang2026records}.


Most existing evidence concerns deterministic regression models evaluated
against reanalysis. Probabilistically trained models, generative ensembles,
and high-resolution regional models have received less attention,
particularly when verified against weather stations, where errors can be
substantially larger
\citep{jin2024weatherreal,gupta2025mausam,wagner2025stationbench}.
Generative models are particularly relevant because they represent a
distribution of possible outcomes rather than a single conditional
estimate, and recent work has shown promising performance for weather
extremes and high-resolution regional forecasting
\citep{price2024gencast,pathak2026stormcast,molinaro2024gencfd}.

A second limitation is that underestimation is often attributed specifically
to AI, even though also NWP models may underestimate the same observations. An
AI model can therefore underestimate an rare event while remaining closer to
it than an NWP forecast. The relevant question is not whether AI models underestimate
extremes, but whether they are less accurate than NWP when extremes occur.

We test this using thirteen forecast models, including ECMWF IFS as the
reference. Forecasts are evaluated over ten months against European
synoptic, solar-radiation, and rain-gauge observations. The analysis covers
10\,m wind, 2\,m temperature, hourly solar radiation, and hourly
precipitation at lead times up to 48\,h. We evaluate extremes in two
complementary ways: first, by stratifying forecasts according to locally
defined climatological regimes; and second, through externally documented
record-breaking temperature events and major European windstorms.

Our main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}

\item We extend station-based evaluation of weather extremes to
deterministic regression models, a CRPS-trained global ensemble, and
high-resolution regional generative models.

\item We distinguish conditional forecast errors from relative accuracy,
testing whether models that lie below high observations are also less
accurate than IFS under the same conditions.

\item We evaluate performance across four surface variables, forecast
leads, countries, and model classes, and complement the climatological
analysis with source-defined temperature-record events, major windstorms,
and categorical verification of heavy precipitation.

\end{itemize}

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:data}

We describe the forecast models and station observations, then introduce the
climatological regimes, bias correction, and evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Models}

Let \(u(t)\) denote the atmospheric state at time \(t\). Weather forecasting
amounts to approximating its evolution from an initial state \(u(t_0)\),
\begin{equation}
\widehat{u}_m(t_0+\tau)
=
\mathcal{F}_m^\tau\bigl(u(t_0)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where \(m\) indexes the forecast model and \(\tau\) is the forecast lead
time. NWP models approximate \(\mathcal{F}_m^\tau\) through numerical
integration of the governing equations on a discrete grid, whereas AI models
learn this mapping from historical atmospheric states. Forecasts may be
deterministic, consisting of a single state, or probabilistic, consisting of
an ensemble,
\[
\left\{\widehat{u}_m^{(k)}(t_0+\tau)\right\}_{k=1}^{K}.
\]

We evaluate twelve forecast models against the high-resolution deterministic
ECMWF IFS forecast, which serves as the reference
(Table~\ref{tab:models}; Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). The comparison spans
NWP and AI models, global and regional domains, and deterministic and
ensemble forecasts.

The NWP models are ECMWF ENS, NOAA GFS, DWD ICON Global, and DWD ICON-EU.
The AI models include ECMWF AIFS \citep{lang2024aifs}, Microsoft Aurora
\citep{bodnar2025aurora}, and Jua EPT-2 Reasoning
\citep{molinaro2025ept2}, all global deterministic regression models. The
global ensembles are ECMWF AIFS ENS \citep{lang2024aifscrps}, a 51-member
ensemble trained with a continuous ranked probability score objective and
learned noise injection, and Jua EPT-2e \citep{molinaro2025ept2}, a
perturbation-based ensemble. The regional models are Jua EPT-2 HRRR
\citep{jua2025hrrr} and EPT-2.1 Europa \citep{jua2026europadocs},
generative ensembles based on the methodology of
\citet{molinaro2026downscaling,molinaro2024gencfd}, and EPT-2.1 Helios
\citep{jua2026helios}, a solar-specialised regional model.

Models are evaluated only for variables with compatible outputs.
Precipitation is available from the NWP models and from EPT-2 Reasoning,
EPT-2e, EPT-2 HRRR, and EPT-2.1 Europa. AIFS, AIFS ENS, and Aurora are
restricted to wind and temperature because their radiation and precipitation
outputs cannot be matched to the hourly verification data.

\subsection{Station observations}
\label{sec:stations}

Forecasts are verified against three complementary in situ datasets:
operational surface observations for 10\,m wind and 2\,m temperature,
solar-radiation observations for hourly global horizontal irradiance, and
observations from 4{,}005 rain gauges for hourly precipitation. Specific
contributing networks and data providers are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:supp}.

\subsection{Observed-value regimes}
\label{sec:thresholds}

Regimes are defined independently of the evaluation period using a fixed
ERA5 1991--2020 climatology \citep{hersbach2020era5}. ERA5 fields are
bilinearly interpolated from the \(0.25^\circ\) grid to each station. For
each station, calendar day, and UTC hour, the reference sample comprises
values from the same UTC hour within a cyclic \(\pm15\)-day window over the
30-year period, yielding 930 values before variable-specific filtering.

Percentile thresholds are computed separately from each reference sample.
Wind and temperature use all reference values, whereas solar radiation uses
positive ERA5 GHI values only. For precipitation, values below
\(0.1\,\mathrm{mm}\) define a separate dry regime, and P50, P75, and P95 are
computed from wet reference hours only. Complete definitions and
meteorological labels are given in Tables~\ref{tab:regimes} and
\ref{tab:precipregimes}.

This local-climatology construction follows established practice for climate
extremes \citep{zhang2011indices,kleintank2009guidelines,
perkins2013heatwaves}. Each verifying observation \(y\) is assigned to a
regime by comparison with the thresholds for its station, calendar day, and
UTC hour.

\subsection{Bias correction}
\label{sec:debias}

Persistent model-wide biases can dominate forecast errors and obscure how
performance changes across observed-value regimes. To reduce their
influence, we apply a causal rolling bias correction before computing skill.
For wind, temperature, and daytime solar radiation, the correction is
additive. Let \(\mathcal{D}_{w,h}\) contain the matched errors at valid hour
\(h\) during the four weeks preceding week \(w\). For model \(m\),
\begin{equation}
\bar b_{m,w,h}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{w,h}|}
\sum_{(f_m,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{w,h}}(f_m-y),
\qquad
\widetilde f_m(t)=f_m(t)-\bar b_{m,w,h(t)}.
\end{equation}
The estimate is pooled across European stations and does not depend on
initialisation or lead. For ensemble models, the same shift is applied to
every member.

An additive correction is unsuitable for zero-bounded, right-skewed
precipitation. Let \(\mathcal{P}_w\) contain forecasts and observations from
the preceding four weeks, pooled across initialisations and leads. We apply
\begin{equation}
a_{m,w}
=
\operatorname{clip}_{[0.33,\,3]}
\left(
\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}y}
     {\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}f_m}
\right),
\qquad
\widetilde f_m(t)=a_{m,w}f_m(t).
\end{equation}
This multiplicative correction adjusts systematic amount biases while
preserving zero forecasts. Both procedures use only prior information and do
not alter observation-defined regime membership or force the conditional
error within each regime to zero.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:aggregation}

We assess forecast accuracy relative to the high-resolution deterministic
ECMWF IFS forecast. For a given variable, let
\(c=(s,t_0,\tau)\) denote an evaluation cell at station \(s\),
initialisation time \(t_0\), and lead time \(\tau\), verified at
\(t_0+\tau\). For model \(m\), let \(\mathcal{C}_m\) denote the set of cells
for which the bias-corrected forecast, the IFS forecast, and the verifying
observation are all available. Each comparison therefore uses
forecast--observation pairs matched in station, initialisation time, lead
time, and valid time.

For the retained cells \(\mathcal{C}_m\), MAE skill relative to IFS is
\begin{equation}
S_{m,\mathcal{C}_m}
=
100\left(
1-\frac{\operatorname{MAE}_{m,\mathcal{C}_m}}
{\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{IFS},\mathcal{C}_m}}
\right).
\end{equation}
Positive skill indicates lower MAE than IFS, whereas negative skill
indicates higher MAE. Initialisation times are restricted to the 00, 06, 12,
and 18\,UTC cycles, and lower-cadence models contribute only at their native
leads.

The evaluation covers thirteen countries: DE, FR, GB, ES, IT, PL, NL, BE,
CH, NO, AT, CZ, and DK. It is organised into two tracks. Track~A covers
1 September 2025 to 30 June 2026. Wind and temperature are evaluated at
6-hourly leads from 6 to 48\,h, whereas solar radiation and hourly
precipitation are evaluated at all leads from 1 to 48\,h. Track~B covers
1 March to 30 June 2026 and compares EPT-2.1 Europa, EPT-2 HRRR, and
ICON-EU on a common hourly schedule at leads from 1 to 48\,h; EPT-2.1 Helios
is additionally included for solar radiation. Italy and Poland are excluded
from precipitation because of missing observations.

Before scoring, forecasts are bilinearly interpolated to station locations
\citep{molinaro2026downscaling}. Precipitation is evaluated only at gauges
within each model's spatial domain, and regional comparisons use a common
gauge sample. Ensemble models are evaluated through their means; the
analysis therefore measures point-forecast accuracy rather than
probabilistic calibration or ensemble spread.

None of the Jua models was trained or fine-tuned on the verification
observations. Historical station errors enter only through the causal
bias-correction procedure described above. Uncertainty in aggregated skill
is quantified using a leave-one-month-out jackknife, with error bars
representing one standard error.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Skill across the observed distribution}
\label{sec:regimes}

\begin{figure}[!hbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{fig3_headline_bars.png}
\caption{Track~A MAE skill relative to ECMWF IFS by observed-value regime,
with \(\pm1\) jackknife SE. Wind and temperature use 6--48\,h leads;
solar radiation uses 1--48\,h.}
\label{fig:bars}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:bars} shows that tail skill does not follow a uniform
AI--physical ordering. EPT-2.1 Europa leads the wind comparison overall
and remains competitive with ICON Global at gale force. EPT-2 HRRR and
EPT-2.1 Europa perform most strongly for temperature, including in the
heat regime. By contrast, AIFS and AIFS ENS remain below IFS in the wind
and temperature tails despite the ensemble's improved performance near
the centre of the distribution. The largest heat deficit belongs to the
physical GFS model.

Solar radiation follows a different pattern. EPT-2.1 Helios leads overall
and under overcast and near-clear-sky conditions but falls below IFS under
typical irradiance, where EPT-2 Reasoning performs most strongly. Its
advantage is therefore concentrated in the tails rather than distributed
uniformly across conditions.

For precipitation, the clearest gains occur in the middle of the wet-hour
distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:precipbars}). EPT-2 HRRR, EPT-2e, and
EPT-2 Reasoning improve on IFS from moderate through high rain, whereas
the physical global models remain below the reference. These gains narrow
at the heaviest intensities. Europa is the exception: its positive
all-conditions score comes from dry hours, while it is worse than IFS in
every wet-hour regime.

We additionally evaluate detection of heavy-rain events using the local
wet-hour P95 threshold. For each forecast--observation pair, the observation
and forecast are each labelled a heavy-rain event if their hourly
accumulation exceeds the same local wet-hour P95 threshold. All evaluated
precipitation models forecast 29--82\% as many P95 exceedances as observed.
Europa and ICON Global nevertheless achieve a slightly higher critical
success index than IFS (Figure~\ref{fig:precipcat};
Table~\ref{tab:precipcat}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figp1_precip_bars.png}
\caption{Track~A MAE skill relative to ECMWF IFS for hourly precipitation
by observed-value regime over 1--48\,h, with \(\pm1\) jackknife SE.}
\label{fig:precipbars}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Shared conditional-error pattern}
\label{sec:fingerprint}

\begin{figure}[!hbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig6_fingerprint.png}
\caption{Mean forecast-minus-observation error at 48\,h, conditioned on
the observed-value regime after bias correction. The grey envelope spans
all models; selected models are highlighted.}
\label{fig:fingerprint}
\end{figure}

Despite their differences in relative skill, all models exhibit the same
conditional-error structure (Figure~\ref{fig:fingerprint}): low
observations are overpredicted and high observations underpredicted. The
variation across regimes is larger than the spread between models and is
present for all four variables. A common tendency towards the centre of
the observed distribution therefore coexists with model-specific
differences in relative accuracy.

Because the regimes are defined using the verifying observations, this
pattern does not by itself show that the models systematically suppress
extreme values. The same relationship can arise from limited
predictability, unresolved local variability, or differences between point
observations and gridded forecasts.

\subsection{Record-breaking temperature events and major windstorms}
\label{sec:events}

To complement the climatological-regime analysis, we evaluate five
externally documented record-breaking temperature events and seven major
European windstorms. Event windows and affected-country footprints were
specified from external reports before scoring. A forecast sample is
retained only when IFS and every Track~A model included in the comparison
share the same station, valid time, initialisation, and 6-hourly lead from
6 to 48\,h. Each model therefore uses identical event windows and
forecast--observation samples. Full event definitions, provenance, scoring
details, and numerical results are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:officialevents} (Tables~\ref{tab:officialeventcatalog}, and ~\ref{tab:officialeventskill}).

Europa shows positive skill in both event sets
(Figure~\ref{fig:officialevents}). During the temperature events,
performance varies substantially across models, with GFS showing the
largest deficit. During the windstorms, most models remain close to IFS,
whereas AIFS, AIFS ENS, and GFS remain below the reference. These
source-defined cases therefore support the model-specific pattern found in
the climatological-regime analysis, rather than a uniform AI-specific loss
of skill during extremes.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig7_official_events.png}
\caption{MAE skill relative to ECMWF IFS during five documented
record-breaking temperature events (left; \(n=231{,}065\)) and seven major
European windstorms (right; \(n=123{,}847\)), aggregated over common
station/valid-time/lead samples from 6--48\,h after bias correction. Error
bars show one leave-one-event-out jackknife SE.}
\label{fig:officialevents}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Regional comparison}

Track~B compares EPT-2.1 Europa and EPT-2 HRRR directly with ICON-EU
over March--June 2026 (Figure~\ref{fig:trackbbars}). Europa retains the
strongest overall wind estimate, while ICON-EU is competitive with the
leading AI models in the wind and temperature tails. EPT-2.1 Helios has
the clearest solar advantage.

The lead-time and 1--12\,h analyses preserve the principal model ordering

temperature and wind patterns are not driven by a single country
(Appendix~\ref{app:country}).


\section{Discussion}

The results distinguish between two questions: whether a model
underestimates an extreme observation, and whether it is less accurate than
IFS when that observation occurs. Across the evaluated models, low
observations are overpredicted and high observations are underpredicted,
although the magnitude of these errors varies. Several AI models are
nevertheless closer to the observations than IFS in the heat, gale, solar,
and precipitation regimes. Underestimation is therefore common to both AI and NWP models, whereas relative accuracy depends on the model, variable, and observed regime.

The same conditional-error pattern appears across deterministic,
probabilistic, and generative AI models, as well as NWP models, with no
consistent ordering between these classes. Instead, differences emerge at
the level of individual systems: AIFS and AIFS ENS perform worse than IFS
in the wind and temperature tails, whereas EPT-2 HRRR and EPT-2.1 Europa
remain competitive at gale force and in heat. The NWP results likewise
vary, with GFS showing the largest heat deficit while ICON-EU is competitive
with the leading regional AI models in the matched Track~B comparison.
The source-defined temperature events and windstorms preserve this
model-specific ordering on common event samples: the leading regional
systems remain ahead of IFS, whereas the two AIFS variants and GFS lose
skill in at least one event set.

Relative accuracy also depends on the observed regime and variable. For
example, EPT-2.1 Helios is more accurate than IFS in both solar tails but
less accurate under typical irradiance. Similarly, EPT-2 HRRR, EPT-2e, and
EPT-2 Reasoning improve on IFS for moderate to high precipitation, although
their advantage narrows for the heaviest events.

Within these scope conditions, the results do not support a general weakness
of AI weather forecasting at extremes.

\subsection{Limitations}

The comparisons identify differences in performance, but not their causes.
The systems differ in architecture, training objective, resolution,
training data, initialisation, cadence, and domain
\citep{bonavita2024limitations,subich2025doublepenalty}; consequently,
their performance cannot be attributed independently to AI versus NWP,
regression versus generative training, or global versus regional
resolution. Moreover, evaluating ensembles through their means assesses
point-forecast accuracy, rather than calibration, spread, or event
probabilities.

\subsection{Track A lead-time, short-range, and precipitation robustness}
\label{app:tracka}

\subsection{Record-breaking temperature events and major windstorms}
\label{app:officialevents}

Table~\ref{tab:officialeventcatalog} lists the externally documented
temperature-record events and European windstorms used in the case-study
analysis. Event windows and affected countries were fixed from the source
catalogue before scoring. Every benchmark station in the source-reported
countries is included at 00, 06, 12, and 18\,UTC throughout the event
window. The analysis is therefore conditioned on documented events, rather
than on whether an individual station observation breaks a record.

For event \(e\) and lead
\(\ell\in\{6,12,\ldots,48\}\,\mathrm{h}\), let
\(\mathcal{C}_{e,\ell}\) denote the station--valid-time pairs \((s,t)\) in
the event footprint for which ECMWF IFS and every Track~A model included in
the comparison provide a forecast initialised at \(t-\ell\). Thus, each
model is evaluated against the same observations at each lead. For ensemble
models, \(f_m\) denotes the ensemble mean and \(\widetilde{f}_m\) the
forecast after the causal bias correction of Section~\ref{sec:debias}. We
compute
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_m(s,t,\ell)
&= \widetilde{f}_m(s,t\mid t-\ell)-y(s,t),\\
N
&= \sum_e \sum_\ell \lvert\mathcal{C}_{e,\ell}\rvert,\\
\operatorname{MAE}^{\mathrm{event}}_m
&= \frac{1}{N}\sum_e\sum_\ell
\sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal{C}_{e,\ell}}
\lvert\varepsilon_m(s,t,\ell)\rvert,\\
S^{\mathrm{event}}_m
&= 100\left(
1-\frac{\operatorname{MAE}^{\mathrm{event}}_m}
{\operatorname{MAE}^{\mathrm{event}}_{\mathrm{IFS}}}
\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:eventskill}
\end{equation}

The same observation may contribute at multiple leads because these are
distinct forecasts from different initialisation times. For uncertainty, we
delete one complete event at a time and report the jackknife standard error
\[
\sqrt{\frac{E-1}{E}\sum_e
\left(S^{\mathrm{event}}_{m,(-e)}
-\overline{S}^{\mathrm{event}}_{m,(-\cdot)}\right)^2},
\]
where \(E=5\) for temperature and \(E=7\) for wind.
Complete pooled values and event-level uncertainty are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:officialeventskill}.
