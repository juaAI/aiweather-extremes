\title{Do AI weather models miss extremes?\\
Evidence from ten months of verification against European
weather stations}

\begin{abstract}
  AI weather models are often reported to underestimate extremes, but most
  evidence concerns deterministic regression models verified against
  reanalysis. We evaluate twelve physical and AI forecast models against
  ECMWF IFS using ten months of European station observations. The
  evaluation covers 10\,m wind, 2\,m temperature, solar radiation, and
  precipitation within regimes defined from a fixed ERA5 1991--2020
  climatology. We find no uniform AI-specific deficit in the tails. Several
  AI models remain more accurate than IFS under extreme conditions, while
  others deteriorate markedly; comparable variation occurs among physical
  models. Every model nevertheless exhibits a common conditional-error
  pattern, overpredicting low observations and underpredicting high
  observations. Attenuation of extreme values therefore does not imply a
  uniform loss of relative skill: tail performance depends on the model,
  variable, and evaluation setting rather than on whether the forecast is
  produced by AI or physical numerical modelling.
  \end{abstract}

  \section{Introduction}

  Data-driven weather models now outperform operational physical models on
  many standard forecast metrics
  \citep{bi2023pangu,lam2023graphcast,bouallegue2024rise}. Their performance
  at extremes remains less certain. Models trained with mean-squared error
  tend towards the conditional mean, potentially smoothing sharp gradients
  and attenuating rare events
  \citep{olivetti2024,bonavita2024limitations}. Evaluations of early AI
  weather models accordingly report underestimated peak winds, hot and
  windy tails, and record-breaking events
  \citep{charltonperez2024,olivetti2024extremes,zhang2026records}.

  Most of this evidence concerns deterministic regression models evaluated
  against reanalysis. Generative ensembles, probabilistically trained
  models, and high-resolution regional AI models have received less
  attention, particularly against direct observations. This distinction
  matters because station-based errors can be substantially larger than
  reanalysis-based estimates
  \citep{jin2024weatherreal,gupta2025mausam,wagner2025stationbench}.

  Recent work also suggests that attenuation is not intrinsic to
  data-driven forecasting. Grid-point regression objectives can reduce
  effective resolution through the double penalty, whereas
  amplitude-preserving losses improve surface-wind extremes
  \citep{subich2025doublepenalty}. Diffusion models preserve flow statistics
  that regression ensembles tend to suppress
  \citep{molinaro2024gencfd}, and generative weather models have demonstrated
  skill for reanalysis-defined extremes and convection-allowing regional
  forecasting \citep{price2024gencast,pathak2026stormcast}. Direct
  station-based comparisons across these model classes nevertheless remain
  limited.

  Two questions are often conflated: whether a forecast attenuates an
  extreme observation and whether it is less accurate than a competing model
  under the same conditions. The first concerns absolute error structure;
  the second concerns relative skill. A model may underestimate an extreme
  while remaining closer to the observation than its physical counterpart.

  We examine this distinction using thirteen forecast models, including
  ECMWF IFS as the reference. Forecasts are verified over ten months against
  European synoptic, solar-radiation, and rain-gauge observations. The
  evaluation covers 10\,m wind, 2\,m temperature, hourly solar radiation,
  and hourly precipitation at lead times up to 48\,h.

  Throughout the paper, ``extreme'' denotes the tails of a local ERA5
  1991--2020 climatology conditioned on station, calendar day, and UTC hour.
  These regimes identify locally unusual conditions rather than necessarily
  record-breaking or impact-defined events. We compare relative MAE within
  these regimes with the corresponding conditional forecast errors. This
  separates model-specific skill from the common tendency of forecasts to
  overpredict low observations and underpredict high observations.
  The study makes three contributions:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item We extend station-based evaluation of weather extremes to a diverse set of forecasting approaches, including deterministic regression models, a CRPS-trained global ensemble, and high-resolution regional generative models.
  \item We disentangle model performance across climatological regimes from the conditional forecast errors within those regimes, separating differences in regime frequency from differences in forecast skill.
  \item We assess the robustness of these patterns across four surface variables, multiple forecast leads, countries, and model classes, and further evaluate heavy precipitation as a categorical extreme.
  \end{itemize}

  \section{Methods}
  \label{sec:data}

  \subsection{Models}

  We evaluate twelve forecast models against the ECMWF IFS high-resolution
  deterministic forecast, which serves as the reference
  (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The comparison spans physical and AI models,
  global and regional domains, and deterministic and ensemble forecasts.

  The physical models are ECMWF ENS, NOAA GFS, DWD ICON Global, and DWD
  ICON-EU. The AI models comprise the global regression models ECMWF AIFS,
  Microsoft Aurora, and Jua EPT-2 Reasoning; the global ensembles ECMWF AIFS
  ENS and Jua EPT-2e; the regional generative ensembles Jua EPT-2 HRRR and
  EPT-2.1 Europa; and the solar-specialised regional model EPT-2.1 Helios
  \citep{lang2024aifs,bodnar2025aurora,molinaro2025ept2,
  lang2024aifscrps,jua2025hrrr,jua2026europadocs,jua2026helios}.
  AIFS ENS is trained with a continuous ranked probability score objective
  and learned noise injection and is evaluated as a 51-member ensemble.
  Related generative methodology is described by
  \citet{molinaro2026downscaling,molinaro2024gencfd}.

  Models are evaluated only for variables with compatible outputs.
  Precipitation is available from the physical models and from EPT-2
  Reasoning, EPT-2e, EPT-2 HRRR, and EPT-2.1 Europa. AIFS ENS is restricted
  to wind and temperature because its radiation and precipitation outputs
  cannot be matched to the hourly verification data.

\subsection{Station observations and evaluation}
  \label{sec:stations}

  Forecasts are verified against three complementary in situ networks: a
  synoptic network for 10\,m wind and 2\,m temperature, a solar network for
  hourly global horizontal irradiance, and a panel of 4{,}005 rain gauges for
  hourly precipitation. The separate gauge network is required because the
  synoptic feed does not provide usable hourly precipitation accumulations.
  Network providers are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:supp}.

  The evaluation covers thirteen countries: DE, FR, GB, ES, IT, PL, NL, BE,
  CH, NO, AT, CZ, and DK. The precipitation network excludes Italy and
  Poland. Forecasts are bilinearly interpolated to observation locations
  \citep{molinaro2026downscaling}. Precipitation is scored only at gauges
  within each model's domain, and regional comparisons use a common gauge
  sample. None of the Jua models was trained or fine-tuned on these
  observations; historical station errors enter only through the causal bias
  correction described below.

  Two evaluation tracks account for differences in availability and output
  cadence. Track~A covers 1 September 2025 to 30 June 2026. Wind and
  temperature use 6-hourly leads from 6 to 48\,h, matching the cadence of
  AIFS, AIFS ENS, and Aurora. Solar radiation and hourly precipitation use
  leads from 1 to 48\,h. Models without compatible outputs are omitted for
  the corresponding variable, and ECMWF ENS contributes to precipitation
  only at its native 6-hourly leads.

  Track~B covers 1 March to 30 June 2026 and compares EPT-2.1 Europa,
  EPT-2 HRRR, and ICON-EU over a common hourly grid from 1 to 48\,h.
  EPT-2.1 Helios is additionally included for solar radiation. Detailed
  availability by variable, country, and period is reported in the
  supplement.

  \subsection{Observed-value regimes}
  \label{sec:thresholds}

  Regimes are defined independently of the evaluation period using the fixed
  ERA5 1991--2020 climatology \citep{hersbach2020era5}. ERA5 fields are
  bilinearly interpolated from the \(0.25^\circ\) grid to each station. For
  every station, calendar day, and UTC hour, the reference distribution
  contains values from a cyclic \(\pm15\)-day window over the 30-year
  period, yielding 930 values before variable-specific conditioning.

  We denote percentiles of this distribution by P5, P25, P50, P75, and
  P95. Wind and temperature use all climatological values, while solar
  percentiles are estimated from positive daytime irradiance.
  Precipitation below \(0.1\)~mm forms a separate dry regime; for
  precipitation, P50, P75, and P95 always denote percentiles of wet hours.
  Each forecast--observation pair is assigned
  to a regime according to its observed value \(y\), so regime membership
  is identical across models and leads. The complete definitions and
  meteorological labels are given in
  Tables~\ref{tab:regimes} and \ref{tab:precipregimes}.

  This local-climatology construction follows established practice for
  climate extremes \citep{zhang2011indices,kleintank2009guidelines,
  perkins2013heatwaves}. Because ERA5 represents grid-cell averages, its
  variance is lower than that of point observations; observed wind and solar
  tails are consequently broader than their nominal climatological
  probabilities.

  \subsection{Bias correction}
  \label{sec:debias}

  We apply a causal rolling bias correction before computing skill. For
  wind, temperature, and daytime solar radiation, the correction is
  additive. Let \(\mathcal{D}_{w,h}\) contain the matched errors at valid
  hour \(h\) during the four weeks preceding week \(w\). For model \(m\),
  \begin{equation}
  \bar b_{m,w,h}
  =
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{w,h}|}
  \sum_{(f_m,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{w,h}}(f_m-y),
  \qquad
  \widetilde f_m(t)=f_m(t)-\bar b_{m,w,h(t)}.
  \end{equation}
  The estimate is pooled across European stations and does not depend on
  initialisation or lead. For ensemble models, the same shift is applied to
  every member.

  An additive correction is unsuitable for zero-bounded, right-skewed
  precipitation. Let \(\mathcal{P}_w\) contain forecasts and observations
  from the preceding four weeks, pooled across initialisations and leads.
  We apply
  \begin{equation}
  a_{m,w}
  =
  \operatorname{clip}_{[0.33,\,3]}
  \left(
  \frac{\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}y}
       {\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}f_m}
  \right),
  \qquad
  \widetilde f_m(t)=a_{m,w}f_m(t).
  \end{equation}
  This multiplicative correction adjusts systematic amount biases while
  preserving zero forecasts. Both procedures use only prior information and
  do not alter observation-defined regime membership or force the
  conditional error within each regime to zero.

  \subsection{Skill and aggregation}
  \label{sec:aggregation}

  For model \(m\) and evaluation cell \(c\), MAE skill relative to ECMWF IFS
  is
  \begin{equation}
  S_{m,c}
  =
  1-\frac{\operatorname{MAE}_{m,c}}
          {\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{IFS},c}}.
  \end{equation}
  Positive values indicate lower MAE than IFS. Scores are computed from
  matched forecast--observation pairs using the common intersection of
  available initialisations and the four daily cycles at 00, 06, 12, and
  18 UTC.

  Ensemble models are evaluated through their ensemble means; the analysis
  therefore assesses point-forecast accuracy rather than calibration or
  spread. Results are reported at selected leads and over the complete lead
  grid of each comparison. Lower-cadence models contribute only at their
  native leads, without temporal interpolation.

  Uncertainty is estimated using a leave-one-month-out jackknife and
  reported as one standard error. For exceedance of
  P95, we additionally report probability of
  detection, false-alarm ratio, critical success index, and frequency bias,
  pooled across gauges and leads up to 48\,h.

  \section{Results}

  \subsection{Skill across the observed distribution}
  \label{sec:regimes}

\begin{figure}[!hbp]
  \centering

  \caption{Track~A MAE skill relative to ECMWF IFS by observed-value regime,
  with \(\pm1\) jackknife SE. Wind and temperature use 6--48\,h leads;
  solar radiation uses 1--48\,h.}
  \label{fig:bars}
  \end{figure}

  Figure~\ref{fig:bars} shows that tail skill does not follow a uniform
  AI--physical ordering. EPT-2.1 Europa leads the wind comparison overall
  and remains competitive with ICON Global at gale force. EPT-2 HRRR and
  EPT-2.1 Europa perform most strongly for temperature, including in the
  heat regime. By contrast, AIFS and AIFS ENS remain below IFS in the wind
  and temperature tails despite the ensemble's improved performance near
  the centre of the distribution. The largest heat deficit belongs to the
  physical GFS model.

  Solar radiation follows a different pattern. EPT-2.1 Helios leads overall
  and under overcast and near-clear-sky conditions but falls below IFS under
  typical irradiance, where EPT-2 Reasoning performs most strongly. Its
  advantage is therefore concentrated in the tails rather than distributed
  uniformly across conditions.


  For precipitation, the clearest gains occur in the middle of the wet-hour
  distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:precipbars}). EPT-2 HRRR, EPT-2e, and
  EPT-2 Reasoning improve on IFS from moderate through high rain, whereas
  the physical global models remain below the reference. These gains narrow
  at the heaviest intensities. Europa follows a distinct pattern, deriving
  much of its aggregate skill from dry hours while losing skill once
  precipitation occurs.

\subsection{Shared conditional-error pattern}
  \label{sec:fingerprint}

\begin{figure}[!hbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig6_fingerprint.png}
  \caption{Mean forecast-minus-observation error at 48\,h, conditioned on
  the observed-value regime after bias correction. The grey envelope spans
  all models; selected models are highlighted.}
  \label{fig:fingerprint}
  \end{figure}

  Despite their differences in relative skill, all models exhibit the same
  conditional-error structure (Figure~\ref{fig:fingerprint}): low
  observations are overpredicted and high observations underpredicted. The
  variation across regimes is larger than the spread between models and is
  present for all four variables. A common tendency towards the centre of
  the observed distribution therefore coexists with model-specific
  differences in relative accuracy.

  Because regimes are conditioned on the verifying observation, this
  pattern is not by itself evidence of smoothing. Limited predictability,
  unresolved local variability, and observational or representativeness
  errors can produce the same structure.


  \subsection{Regional comparison}

  Track~B compares EPT-2.1 Europa and EPT-2 HRRR directly with ICON-EU
  over March--June 2026 (Figure~\ref{fig:trackbbars}). Europa retains the
  strongest overall wind estimate, while ICON-EU is competitive with the
  leading AI models in the wind and temperature tails. EPT-2.1 Helios has
  the clearest solar advantage.

  \section{Discussion}

  The results distinguish two meanings of ``missing extremes.'' A forecast
  may underestimate an extreme observation, or it may be less accurate than
  a competing model under the same conditions. The first behaviour is
  common: all evaluated models tend to overpredict low observations and
  underpredict high observations. The second is not. Several AI models
  achieve lower MAE than IFS in the heat, gale, solar, and precipitation
  regimes. Attenuation of extreme values therefore does not imply a general
  AI-specific deficit in relative skill.

  This distinction qualifies earlier findings based mainly on deterministic
  regression models evaluated against reanalysis
  \citep{olivetti2024extremes}. The broader station-based comparison reveals
  substantial variation across models and variables. EPT-2.1 Helios performs
  strongly in both solar tails but loses skill under typical irradiance,
  while the precipitation gains of the Jua models are concentrated in the
  middle of the wet distribution. Tail performance is therefore not
  determined by aggregate skill alone.

  The results are also inconsistent with a simple AI--physical divide. The
  wind- and temperature-tail deficits of AIFS persist for AIFS ENS despite
  its improved central-regime performance. Conversely, EPT-2 HRRR and
  EPT-2.1 Europa remain competitive at gale force and in heat. Physical
  models show comparable variation: GFS has the largest heat deficit, while
  ICON-EU is competitive with the leading regional AI models.

  These comparisons do not establish the causes of the differences. The
  models vary in architecture, objective, resolution, training data,
  initialisation, cadence, and domain
  \citep{bonavita2024limitations,subich2025doublepenalty}. Their performance
  cannot therefore be attributed independently to AI versus physics,
  regression versus generative training, or global versus regional
  resolution. Evaluating ensembles through their means also provides no
  evidence about calibration, spread, or extreme-event probabilities.

  Absolute attenuation and relative skill are therefore complementary.
  AI models may underestimate extreme observations without being uniformly
  less accurate than physical models, while strong aggregate performance
  does not guarantee an advantage in every tail. Within this evaluation,
  performance at extremes is model- and variable-specific rather than a
  general weakness of AI weather forecasting.
