\title{Do AI weather models miss extremes?\\
Evidence from ten months of verification against European
weather stations}

\begin{abstract}
  AI weather models are often reported to underestimate extremes, but most
  evidence concerns deterministic regression models verified against
  reanalysis. We evaluate twelve physical and AI forecast models against
  ECMWF IFS using ten months of European station observations. The
  evaluation covers 10\,m wind, 2\,m temperature, solar radiation, and
  precipitation, with observed-value regimes defined from a fixed ERA5
  1991--2020 climatology and skill computed on matched samples. We find no
  uniform AI-specific deficit in the tails. Several AI models remain more
  accurate than IFS under extreme conditions, while others deteriorate
  markedly; comparable variation is present among physical models. These
  model-specific differences persist across variables and supporting
  lead-time and regional comparisons. At the same time, every model exhibits
  a common observation-conditioned error pattern, overpredicting low values
  and underpredicting high values. Attenuation of extreme observations
  therefore does not imply a uniform loss of relative skill: performance in
  the tails depends on the individual model, variable, and evaluation
  setting rather than on whether a forecast is produced by AI or physical
  numerical modelling.
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Data-driven weather models now outperform operational physical models on
many standard forecast metrics
\citep{bi2023pangu,lam2023graphcast,bouallegue2024rise}. Their performance
at extremes, however, remains an open concern. Models trained with
mean-squared error tend towards the conditional mean, which can smooth
sharp gradients and attenuate rare events
\citep{olivetti2024,bonavita2024limitations}. Existing evaluations support
this concern, reporting underestimated peak winds, hot and windy tails,
and record-breaking events
\citep{charltonperez2024,olivetti2024extremes,zhang2026records}.

The available evidence has two important limitations. First, it is
dominated by first-generation deterministic regression models. Generative
ensembles, probabilistically trained models, and high-resolution regional
AI models have received much less attention. Second, most studies verify
forecasts against reanalysis rather than direct station observations.
Whether their conclusions extend to newer model classes and independent
in situ measurements is therefore unclear.

Two questions are often conflated in this discussion: whether forecasts
systematically attenuate extreme observations, and whether AI models are
less accurate than physical models under the same conditions. The first
concerns absolute forecast error; the second concerns relative skill. A
model may underestimate an extreme event while still producing a smaller
error than its physical counterpart.

We examine these questions using thirteen forecast models, including ECMWF
IFS as the reference. Forecasts are verified over ten months against
European synoptic, solar-radiation, and rain-gauge observations. The
evaluation covers 10\,m wind, 2\,m temperature, hourly solar radiation,
and hourly precipitation at lead times up to 48\,h. Models are compared
both overall and within regimes defined by the observed value relative to
a fixed 1991--2020 climatology.

Throughout the paper, ``extreme'' refers to the tails of this local
climatological distribution, principally observations below the 5th or
above the 95th quantile. These regimes identify conditions that are
unusual for a given location, calendar day, and UTC hour; they are not
necessarily record-breaking or impact-defined events. Precipitation is
treated separately within the same framework by distinguishing dry hours
and quantiles of the wet-hour distribution
\citep{zhang2011indices,perkins2013heatwaves}.

The analysis reveals a common tendency among AI and physical models to
overpredict low observations and underpredict high observations. Relative
skill within those regimes, however, varies substantially by model and
variable. This distinction is central: attenuation of extreme values does
not imply a uniform AI-specific deficit relative to physical forecasting.

The study makes three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item It extends station-based evaluation of weather extremes to
deterministic regression models, a CRPS-trained global ensemble, and
high-resolution regional generative models.

\item It separates relative MAE within climatological regimes from the
conditional forecast errors observed in those regimes.

\item It tests whether these patterns persist across four surface
variables, forecast lead times, countries, and model classes, with
additional categorical evaluation of heavy precipitation.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the verification protocol.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Evaluations of early AI weather models report competitive average skill
alongside attenuated extremes. FourCastNet, Pangu-Weather, and GraphCast
underestimated peak winds during storm Ciar\'an despite reproducing its
track and central pressure \citep{charltonperez2024}. Broader studies
similarly identify deficits in hot, windy, and record-breaking events,
particularly at longer lead times
\citep{olivetti2024extremes,zhang2026records}. These errors have been
linked to reduced effective resolution and the suppression of
sub-synoptic variability \citep{bonavita2024limitations}. However,
station-based evidence suggests a more qualified picture: performance can
remain competitive for moderate extremes while degrading for the most
intense events \citep{bouallegue2024rise}. Existing evidence is therefore
concentrated on deterministic regression models evaluated primarily
against reanalysis.

Recent work indicates that attenuation may depend on the training
objective rather than on data-driven forecasting itself. The grid-point
mean-squared-error objective can produce a double penalty that reduces
effective resolution, while amplitude-preserving objectives improve
surface-wind extremes \citep{subich2025doublepenalty}. Diffusion-based
models likewise preserve flow statistics that ensembles of regression
models tend to suppress \citep{molinaro2024gencfd}. At weather scales,
GenCast improves on the ECMWF ensemble for ERA5-defined extremes, and
StormCast demonstrates generative modelling at convection-allowing
resolution \citep{price2024gencast,pathak2026stormcast}. Direct
station-based comparisons across regression, probabilistic, generative,
and physical models nevertheless remain limited.

WeatherReal, MAUSAM, and StationBench provide observational benchmarks for
data-driven weather forecasting
\citep{jin2024weatherreal,gupta2025mausam,wagner2025stationbench}.
Their results show that verification against reanalysis can overstate
performance relative to in situ observations. We build on this work by
comparing climatological tail skill across model classes using independent
European station observations.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Models}

We evaluate twelve forecast models against the ECMWF IFS high-resolution
deterministic forecast, which serves as the reference
(Table~\ref{tab:models}). The comparison spans physical and AI models,
global and regional domains, and deterministic and ensemble forecasts.

The physical models are ECMWF ENS, NOAA GFS, DWD ICON Global, and DWD
ICON-EU. The AI models comprise the global regression models ECMWF AIFS,
Microsoft Aurora, and Jua EPT-2 Reasoning; the global ensembles ECMWF AIFS
ENS and Jua EPT-2e; the regional generative ensembles Jua EPT-2 HRRR and
EPT-2.1 Europa; and the solar-specialised regional model EPT-2.1 Helios
\citep{lang2024aifs,bodnar2025aurora,molinaro2025ept2,
lang2024aifscrps,jua2025hrrr,jua2026europadocs,jua2026helios}.
AIFS ENS retains the AIFS architecture but is trained with a continuous
ranked probability score objective and learned noise injection; it is
evaluated as a 51-member ensemble. Related generative methodology is
described by \citet{molinaro2026downscaling,molinaro2024gencfd}.

Models are evaluated only for variables with compatible outputs.
Precipitation is available from the physical models and from EPT-2
Reasoning, EPT-2e, EPT-2 HRRR, and EPT-2.1 Europa. AIFS ENS is restricted
to 10\,m wind and 2\,m temperature because its radiation and precipitation
outputs cannot be matched to the hourly accumulations used for
verification.

\subsection{Station observations and evaluation}
\label{sec:stations}

\paragraph{Station data.}
Forecasts are verified against three complementary in situ networks:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Synoptic:} quality-controlled observations of 10\,m wind and
2\,m temperature.

\item \textbf{Solar radiation:} hourly global horizontal irradiance from
DWD, M\'et\'eo-France, MeteoSwiss, KNMI, RMI, DMI, MET Norway (Frost),
GeoSphere Austria, SMHI, FMI, BSRN, and ARPA Lombardia.

\item \textbf{Precipitation:} hourly accumulations from 4{,}005 rain gauges
operated by DWD, M\'et\'eo-France, MET Norway, AEMET, GeoSphere Austria,
the UK Met Office, KNMI, RMI, CHMI, DMI, and MeteoSwiss. This separate
network is required because the synoptic feed does not provide usable
hourly precipitation accumulations.
\end{itemize}

The evaluation covers thirteen countries: DE, FR, GB, ES, IT, PL, NL, BE,
CH, NO, AT, CZ, and DK. The precipitation network excludes Italy and
Poland. Forecasts are bilinearly interpolated to observation locations
\citep{molinaro2026downscaling}. Precipitation is scored only at gauges
within each model's domain, and regional comparisons use a common gauge
sample within each evaluation cell.

None of the Jua models was trained or fine-tuned on these observations.
Historical station errors enter the evaluation only through the causal
bias correction described in Section~\ref{sec:debias}.

\paragraph{Evaluation tracks.}
Two tracks account for differences in model availability and output
cadence.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Track A} covers 1 September 2025 to 30 June 2026. Wind and
temperature are evaluated for IFS and the ten comparator models available
throughout this period; ICON-EU is reserved for Track~B. Leads range from
6 to 48\,h at 6-hourly intervals, matching the cadence of AIFS, AIFS ENS,
and Aurora.

Solar radiation is evaluated over the same period at hourly leads from
1 to 48\,h. EPT-2.1 Helios is included, while AIFS, AIFS ENS, Aurora, and
ECMWF ENS are omitted because they lack compatible hourly solar output.
The solar network contains no stations in GB, ES, or PL. Italian coverage,
provided mainly by ARPA Lombardia, ends in February 2026; subsequent solar
results therefore exclude Italy.

Precipitation is evaluated from 1 to 48\,h for models with compatible
output. Hourly models contribute at every lead, whereas ECMWF ENS
contributes only at its native 6-hourly cadence.

\item \textbf{Track B} covers 1 March to 30 June 2026 and compares
EPT-2.1 Europa, EPT-2 HRRR, and ICON-EU over a common hourly lead grid
from 1 to 48\,h. All four variables are evaluated, with EPT-2.1 Helios
additionally included for solar radiation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Observed-value regimes}
\label{sec:thresholds}

Regimes are defined from the fixed 1991--2020 ERA5 climatology
\citep{hersbach2020era5}, independently of the evaluation period. ERA5
fields are bilinearly interpolated from the \(0.25^\circ\) grid to each
station. For each station, calendar day, and UTC hour, the reference
distribution comprises values from a cyclic \(\pm15\)-day window over the
30-year period. This yields 930 values before variable-specific
conditioning and preserves seasonal and diurnal variability.

Wind and temperature use the complete reference distribution. Solar
radiation and precipitation require additional conditioning because both
contain a structural mass at zero. Solar thresholds are estimated from
positive daytime GHI. For precipitation, observations below \(0.1\)~mm
form a separate dry regime, and the remaining thresholds are estimated
from wet hours.

Let \(q_\alpha(s,d,h)\) denote the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the relevant
reference distribution at station \(s\), calendar day \(d\), and UTC hour
\(h\). Wind, temperature, and solar radiation use \(q_{0.05}\),
\(q_{0.25}\), \(q_{0.75}\), and \(q_{0.95}\). Precipitation uses
\(q_{0.50}^{\mathrm{wet}}\), \(q_{0.75}^{\mathrm{wet}}\), and
\(q_{0.95}^{\mathrm{wet}}\). We omit the dependence on station, day, and
hour below.

Each forecast--observation pair is assigned to a regime according to its
observed value \(y\), so membership is identical across models and lead
times. Tables~\ref{tab:regimes} and \ref{tab:precipregimes} give the
corresponding intervals and meteorological labels.

The use of a fixed local climatology follows established practice in the
climate-extremes literature
\citep{zhang2011indices,kleintank2009guidelines,
perkins2013heatwaves,perkins2015review}; the 1991--2020 period follows the
WMO climate-normal convention. Conditioning precipitation quantiles on
wet hours is consistent with ETCCDI wet-day indices such as R95p.

Because thresholds are estimated independently of the evaluation period,
observed regime frequencies need not equal their nominal probabilities.
Moreover, ERA5 grid-cell values have lower spatial variance than point
observations, producing broader wind and solar tails than a station-based
climatology. The primary regimes of interest are the upper wind and
temperature tails, both solar tails, and the upper wet-hour precipitation
tail.

\subsection{Bias correction}
\label{sec:debias}

We apply a causal rolling bias correction before computing the reported
scores. For wind, temperature, and daytime solar radiation, the correction
is additive. Let \(w\) denote a valid week, \(h\) the UTC hour, and
\(\mathcal{D}_{w,h}\) the matched forecast--observation pairs at hour \(h\)
during the preceding four weeks. For model \(m\),
\begin{equation}
\bar b_{m,w,h}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{w,h}|}
\sum_{(f_m,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{w,h}}(f_m-y),
\qquad
\widetilde f_m(t)=f_m(t)-\bar b_{m,w,h(t)}.
\end{equation}
The estimate is pooled across European stations and does not depend on
initialisation or lead time. It is estimated separately for each model,
and the same shift is applied to every ensemble member, preserving spread.

An additive correction is unsuitable for precipitation because the
distribution is zero-bounded and strongly right-skewed. Let
\(\mathcal{P}_w\) contain precipitation forecasts and observations from the
preceding four weeks, pooled across initialisation hours and lead times.
Forecasts valid during week \(w\) are corrected by
\begin{equation}
a_{m,w}
=
\operatorname{clip}_{[0.33,\,3]}
\left(
\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}y}
     {\sum_{\mathcal{P}_w}f_m}
\right),
\qquad
\widetilde f_m(t)=a_{m,w}f_m(t).
\end{equation}
This multiplicative correction adjusts systematic wet or dry amount biases
while preserving zero forecasts.

Both corrections use only information available before the valid week.
Because regimes are assigned from observations, the correction does not
alter regime membership. It removes recent mean bias but does not force
the conditional bias within each regime to vanish.

\subsection{Skill, lead times, and aggregation}
\label{sec:aggregation}

Let \(m\) denote a forecast model and \(c\) an evaluation cell defined by
track, country, variable, lead time, and regime. MAE skill relative to
ECMWF IFS is
\begin{equation}
S_{m,c}
=
1-\frac{\operatorname{MAE}_{m,c}}
        {\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{IFS},c}}.
\end{equation}
Positive values indicate lower MAE than IFS. The numerator and denominator
are computed from matched forecast--observation pairs, using a common
intersection of available initialisations and the four daily cycles at
00, 06, 12, and 18 UTC.

Ensemble models are evaluated through their ensemble means. The analysis
therefore compares point-forecast accuracy and does not assess ensemble
spread or probabilistic calibration. Mean forecast-minus-observation error
is also reported within each regime.

Results are shown at 1, 6, 12, 24, and 48\,h where available and are also
aggregated over the lead grid of each comparison. Track~A wind and
temperature use 6-hourly leads from 6 to 48\,h. Track~A solar radiation,
Track~B, and hourly precipitation models use hourly leads from 1 to
48\,h. Lower-cadence models contribute only at their native leads; no
temporal interpolation is applied.

Uncertainty is estimated by a leave-one-month-out jackknife and reported
as one standard error. For heavy precipitation, defined by exceedance of
\(q_{0.95}^{\mathrm{wet}}\), we additionally report probability of
detection, false-alarm ratio, critical success index, and frequency bias.
These statistics are computed from forecast and observed exceedances
pooled across gauges and lead times up to 48\,h.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Skill across the observed distribution}
\label{sec:regimes}

Figure~\ref{fig:bars} shows that relative skill in the tails does not
follow a uniform AI--physical ordering. EPT-2.1 Europa leads the wind
comparison overall and remains competitive with ICON Global at gale
force. EPT-2 HRRR and EPT-2.1 Europa perform most strongly for
temperature, including in the heat regime. By contrast, AIFS and AIFS ENS
remain below IFS in the wind and temperature tails, despite the ensemble's
improved performance near the centre of the distribution. The largest
heat deficit belongs to the physical GFS model.

Solar radiation exhibits a different pattern. EPT-2.1 Helios leads
overall and under overcast and near-clear-sky conditions, but falls below
IFS in the typical irradiance regime, where EPT-2 Reasoning performs most
strongly. Its advantage is therefore concentrated in the tails rather
than distributed uniformly across conditions.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{fig3_headline_bars.png}
\caption{Track~A MAE skill relative to ECMWF IFS by observed-value regime,
with \(\pm1\) jackknife SE. Wind and temperature use 6--48\,h leads;
solar radiation uses 1--48\,h.}
\label{fig:bars}
\end{figure}

For precipitation, the clearest gains occur in the middle of the wet-hour
distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:precipbars}). EPT-2 HRRR, EPT-2e, and
EPT-2 Reasoning improve substantially on IFS from moderate through high
rain, whereas the physical global models remain below the reference.
These gains narrow at the heaviest intensities: EPT-2 Reasoning remains
slightly ahead, EPT-2.1 Europa is close to IFS, and EPT-2 HRRR and EPT-2e
fall slightly below it. Europa follows a distinct pattern, deriving much
of its aggregate skill from dry hours while losing skill once
precipitation occurs.

\subsection{Shared conditional-error pattern}
\label{sec:fingerprint}

Despite their differences in relative skill, all models exhibit the same
conditional-error structure (Figure~\ref{fig:fingerprint}): low
observations are overpredicted and high observations underpredicted. The
variation across regimes is considerably larger than the spread between
models and is present for all four variables. A shared tendency towards
the centre of the observed distribution therefore coexists with
model-specific differences in relative accuracy.

Because the regimes are conditioned on the verifying observation, this
pattern is not in itself evidence of model smoothing. Limited
predictability, unresolved local variability, and observational or
representativeness errors can produce the same structure.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig6_fingerprint.png}
\caption{Mean forecast-minus-observation error at 48\,h, conditioned on
the observed-value regime after bias correction. The grey envelope spans
all models. ECMWF IFS, EPT-2.1 Europa, and ECMWF AIFS are highlighted in
every panel; the solar panel additionally highlights EPT-2.1 Helios and
the precipitation panel EPT-2 Reasoning.}
\label{fig:fingerprint}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Robustness analyses}
\label{sec:robustness}

The main conclusions persist across lead times
(Figures~\ref{fig:leadwind}--\ref{fig:preciplead}), the 1--12\,h range
(Figures~\ref{fig:bars12} and \ref{fig:precipbars12}), individual
countries (Figures~\ref{fig:appbwind}--\ref{fig:appbprecip}), and the
matched regional comparison
(Figures~\ref{fig:trackbbars}--\ref{fig:preciptrackblead}). ICON-EU is
competitive with the leading AI models in the wind and temperature tails,
while the principal wind, solar, and precipitation patterns remain
unchanged. Complete robustness and categorical-precipitation results
(Figure~\ref{fig:precipcat}, Table~\ref{tab:precipcat}) are reported in
the supplement.
\section{Discussion}

The results distinguish two meanings of ``missing extremes.'' A forecast
may underestimate the magnitude of an extreme observation, or it may be
less accurate than a competing model under the same conditions. The first
behaviour is common across the evaluated models: low observations tend to
be overpredicted and high observations underpredicted. The second is not.
Several AI models achieve lower MAE than IFS within the heat, gale, solar,
and precipitation regimes. Attenuation of extreme values therefore does
not imply a general AI-specific deficit in relative skill.

This distinction qualifies earlier findings based primarily on
first-generation regression models verified against reanalysis
\citep{olivetti2024extremes}. The broader, station-based comparison
considered here reveals substantial variation across models and variables.
EPT-2.1 Helios performs strongly in both solar tails but loses skill under
typical irradiance, while the Jua precipitation models improve most clearly
through the middle of the wet distribution rather than at its extreme.
Performance in the tails is therefore not determined by aggregate skill
alone.

The results are also inconsistent with a simple AI--physical divide. The
wind- and temperature-tail deficits of AIFS persist for AIFS ENS despite
its improved central-regime performance. In contrast, EPT-2 HRRR and
EPT-2.1 Europa remain competitive at gale force and in heat. Among the
physical models, ECMWF ENS loses skill in the heat regime, GFS has the
largest heat deficit, and ICON-EU is competitive with the leading regional
AI models. These differences are better understood as properties of
individual models than of their broad forecasting paradigm.

The comparison does not establish what causes these differences. The
models vary simultaneously in architecture, training objective,
resolution, training data, initialisation, forecast cadence, and spatial
domain \citep{bonavita2024limitations,subich2025doublepenalty}.
Performance cannot therefore be attributed independently to AI versus
physics, regression versus generative training, or global versus regional
resolution. Moreover, evaluating ensembles through their means assesses
point-forecast accuracy but not calibration, spread, or extreme-event
probabilities.

Absolute attenuation and relative skill are thus complementary properties.
AI models may underestimate extreme observations without being uniformly
less accurate than physical models, just as strong aggregate performance
does not guarantee an advantage in every tail. Within this evaluation,
performance at extremes is model- and variable-specific rather than a
general property of AI weather forecasting.
